# Comparison Audit

**One page. Fill it in before class. Bring it with you.**

You will do this for every assigned reading, and later for your own project. The point is not to
summarize what you read. It is to find the comparison inside it and work out what that comparison
can actually support.

Most of these are short answers. If a question can't be answered from the reading, write that —
"they never say" is a real finding, and often the most important one on the page.

---

### 1. What did they want to know?

The question, in one sentence, in your own words. Not the title.

### 2. What kind of answer is it?

Pick one and say how you can tell.

- **What happened** — how much, where, to whom
- **How it compares** — is this group higher or lower than that one
- **What caused it** — did X change Y
- **What's coming** — what will happen next
- **What to do** — a recommendation

If you can't fill in both rows, say so. A missing comparison group is the most common problem you
will find.

### 4. Could the data answer this question?

- **What is one row in their data?** (One person? One arrest? One city-year?)
- **Is the time detail fine enough?** A question about seasons cannot be answered with one number
  per year.
- **Does what they measured mean what the question needs it to mean?** Arrests are not crimes.
  Complaints are not noise. A clearance rate is not a solve rate.

### 5. What has to be true for this to be a fair comparison?

Write the assumption in one sentence. Then name **one specific thing** that could make it untrue —
not "other factors," but one named thing.

### 6. What would change your mind?

What result, comparison, or piece of evidence would make you doubt the finding?

### 7. Rewrite the headline.

Write the strongest sentence the comparison actually supports. If the original already gets it
right, say so and explain why it does.

## A worked example

From the precinct exercise in our first Python notebook:

> **1. What did they want to know?** Which of two precincts had more arrests last week.
>
> **2. What kind of answer?** How it compares — two groups, one measure.
>
> **3. Compared with what?** Group A: Precinct 1, last week, daily arrest counts. Group B: Precinct
> 2, same week. Measured: total arrests. Per what: **nothing — these are raw counts.**
>
> **4. Could the data answer it?** One row is one day in one precinct. Time detail is fine for a
> weekly question. But arrests measure police activity as much as crime, and nothing here accounts
> for how many people live in each precinct or how many officers work there.
>
> **5. What has to be true?** That the two precincts are alike in every way that affects arrests
> except the thing we care about. One specific thing that could make that untrue: Precinct 1 might
> simply be larger.
>
> **6. What would change my mind?** Arrests per 1,000 residents instead of raw counts. If the gap
> disappears, it was population, not crime.
>
> **7. Rewrite the headline.** Not "Precinct 1 is more dangerous." Try: "Precinct 1 recorded more
> arrests than Precinct 2 last week. We do not yet know whether that reflects more crime, more
> people, or more officers."

Notice that the analysis was correct and the first headline was still wrong. That gap is what this
page is for.

## Using this for your own project

Same seven questions, turned around. Update it every couple of weeks as your question sharpens:

1. What do I want to know?
2. What kind of answer am I after?
3. What am I comparing with what?
4. Can the data I actually have answer it?
5. What has to be true for my comparison to be fair?
6. What would change my mind?
7. What can I honestly say right now?

Then add one line: **what changed since last time, and why.** Usually a reading or a class
discussion will have moved something. The proposal is just this page once it stops changing much.
